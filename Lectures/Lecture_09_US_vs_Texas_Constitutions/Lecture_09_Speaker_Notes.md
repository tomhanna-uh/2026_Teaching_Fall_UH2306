# Lecture 9 Speaker Notes — US vs Texas Constitutions (UH only)

**Dates:** September 23 / 24, 2026  
**Timing target:** ~50 minutes core + ~15 minutes Top Hat

---

## Empowerment

Students should leave able to: (1) explain rights-first (Texas Article I) vs powers-first then amendments (U.S.), (2) use the "two articles behind" pattern as a reliable map, (3) connect length of the Texas Constitution to residual state power under federalism, (4) contrast unitary vs plural executive and unitary vs dual high courts.

---

## Historical open (spoken heavy)

Four rebellions / conflicts in the cultural memory of 1876: Mexican independence, American Revolution inheritance, Texas Revolution, Civil War and Reconstruction. Result: even more suspicion of concentrated power than the U.S. Founders. The 1876 text is written in the language of rights of "the people" — a frame we now apply far more equally across race and other lines than the drafters did. Keep this spoken; slides stay thin.

---

## Two articles behind (spine)

Return to the map with every branch:

| U.S. | Texas |
|------|--------|
| I Legislature | III Legislature |
| II Executive | IV Executive |
| III Judiciary | V Judiciary |

Why? Bill of Rights is Article I; powers of government / separation is Article II.

Early Top Hat: Bill of Rights is Article I.  
Late Top Hat: conceptual — prioritizes limiting government; lag exists because rights and powers-of-government occupy I and II.

---

## Size / federalism

Pamphlet vs book visual. ~20× length is the punch line. Short federal + long state is the federal design working as intended. Light touch on extra-constitutional federal activity; education as the clean example of silence in the U.S. text.

---

## Branches (condensed)

**Legislature (III):** biennial, 140-day regular session, low pay, Comptroller certification of revenue.

**Executive (IV):** plural — six Article IV elected offices; Railroad Commission also elected (oil/gas). No current officeholder names on slides.

**Judiciary (V):** dual high courts (SCOTX civil; Court of Criminal Appeals criminal); elected judges; fixed terms vs federal appointment/life tenure.

---

## Texas-specific principles

Homestead protection; no state personal income tax — examples of putting policy limits in the constitution so they are harder to change than statutes.
