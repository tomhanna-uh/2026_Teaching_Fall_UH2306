# Speaker Notes — UH Lecture 15: Trade-offs and Unintended Consequences

## Empowerment
Emphasize recognition and the beginning of understanding. Students will not become policy experts in one day; the goal is pattern recognition and a habit of asking “what is the other side of this ledger?”

## Federalist 10 / Angels bridge
Madison’s line is usually read as a comment on human nature (corruption, ambition). Push the further point: even if every official were perfectly virtuous, the knowledge problem remains. No one can know all the local, tacit, time-sensitive information needed to design complex interventions without error.

## Knowledge problem and trade-offs
These are distinct but related. Trade-offs exist even with perfect knowledge (you still cannot have everything). The knowledge problem makes the trade-offs harder to see in advance and makes unintended consequences more likely. Voters and officials both face this; concentrated interests often see their side more clearly than diffuse costs.

## Personal trade-off opener
Use the $3 candy-bar-or-soda example (or any parallel). In a large section this can be rhetorical or a quick Top Hat poll (“Which would you buy?”) before moving on.

## Hancock and Garner
Pair them deliberately. Hancock is celebrated; Garner is tragic. The common element is high taxes on a desired good + enforcement by force. The human cost of enforcement is part of the true price of the policy.

## Unintended consequences as a form of trade-off
Students sometimes treat “unintended” as pure surprise. Frame it as: every action has more effects than the ones we intend; those extra effects are still costs (or, occasionally, benefits) that belong on the ledger.

## Cobra effect
Classic, memorable, non-partisan. Use it as the clean visual for the general pattern.

## Sin taxes / black markets
Link back to enforcement costs and to the free-rider / taxation discussion from earlier in the course if time allows.

## DDT and three-strikes
Keep short. The point is the incentive change, not a full history of either policy.

## “Sometimes better” + Texas secession / Emancipation
This is the slide pair you asked for. Present the historical sequence neutrally: the secession vote was intended to protect slavery; the war that followed produced emancipation. You can decide in the moment whether to develop the ethical-voting parallel to the later Ethics of the Citizen lecture. If you drop it, the preceding “sometimes better” slide still works with other examples. Texas content is appropriate here.

## Laffer and inequality math
Laffer: the curve exists; the location of the peak is empirical and contested. Do not claim any particular current rate is on the wrong side.

Inequality graphic: stress the pure mathematical point first (proportional growth enlarges absolute gaps while raising everyone’s absolute position). Then note that real-world income growth also changes relative positions for many other reasons. The graphic is the foundation, not the whole story.

## Short-term vs long-term and concentrated benefits
These two visuals support the political-economy point: democratic and interest-group pressures often favor visible near-term benefits and concentrated gains over diffuse or delayed costs.

## Road to Serfdom spiral
Hayek’s core insight for this lecture: the response to the failure of one intervention is often another intervention, not repeal. The spiral is the visual cue.

## Top Hat
Prepare a short bank:
- T/F: “If government officials were perfectly honest and well-intentioned, unintended consequences would disappear.”
- MC: best definition of a trade-off; best example of an unintended consequence; which graphic illustrates concentrated benefits / diffuse costs.
- Likert: “When evaluating a new policy I should focus mainly on the problem it claims to solve.”
- Optional short free-response text bank for AI generation if needed.

## Closing
Return to the course spine: we limit government not only because of the danger of force, but because of the limits of knowledge and the reality of trade-offs. Transition cleanly to the Executive lecture.
